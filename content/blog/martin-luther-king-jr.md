It is easy to remember Martin Luther King Jr. as a statue: a fixed figure, always 34, always on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial. It is more useful to remember how young he was, and how fast everything happened.

## At 26: Montgomery

On 1 December 1955, Rosa Parks was arrested in Montgomery, Alabama, for refusing to give up her seat on a city bus to a white passenger. Within days, Black residents organised a boycott of the buses, and they chose a 26-year-old pastor, new to the city, to lead it.

The boycott lasted 381 days. People walked to work, shared cars and organised their own transport, and they kept going through arrests and the bombing of King's home. It ended after the Supreme Court ruled Montgomery's bus segregation unconstitutional. The organising behind it became a model for the movement that followed.

## A stop in Accra

In March 1957, a few months after the boycott ended, King and his wife Coretta travelled to Accra to attend Ghana's independence celebrations as guests. He watched the Union Jack come down and a new African nation stand up. He came home talking about it, seeing Ghana's independence and the American freedom struggle as part of the same story. It's a connection people in Ghana don't hear about often enough.

## At 34: Washington

By 1963, King was the best-known face of the civil rights movement. That spring, he was jailed during the campaign against segregation in Birmingham, Alabama, and wrote his "Letter from Birmingham Jail," answering the clergymen who had told him to wait. Its argument still stands: those who benefit from injustice will always say the time is not right.

On 28 August 1963, he stood before roughly a quarter of a million people at the March on Washington and gave the speech the world now knows by its refrain.

The following year, the Civil Rights Act of 1964 outlawed segregation in public places and discrimination in employment. In October 1964, at 35, King became the youngest person at the time to receive the Nobel Peace Prize. That same month, speaking at Oberlin College, he said the line I chose for the top of this site: "The time is always right to do what is right." In 1965, after the marches from Selma, came the Voting Rights Act.

## At 39: Memphis

In his last years, King widened his focus to poverty and to the Vietnam War. Both positions cost him allies, and his approval ratings fell. He was in Memphis, Tennessee, supporting striking sanitation workers, when he was shot and killed on 4 April 1968. He was 39.

It took until 1986 for the United States to first observe a federal holiday in his name.

## What I take from it

King did not wait to be older, more established or more certain before acting. He was 26 when people asked him to lead and 39 when he died, and the work happened in between. Most of us are somewhere inside that age range right now.

That's the reason his line sits at the top of this site. Not as decoration, but as a reminder that there is no better time coming.
