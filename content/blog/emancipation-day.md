On 1 August 1834, the Slavery Abolition Act passed by the British Parliament the year before came into force across most of Britain's colonies. On paper, roughly 800,000 enslaved people in the Caribbean, South Africa and elsewhere stopped being property that morning.

On paper. In practice, most of them were moved straight into a system called "apprenticeship," which bound them to unpaid work for their former owners for several more years. Full freedom in the British Caribbean only came in 1838. And the £20 million the British government paid out as compensation went to the slave owners, for the loss of their "property." The people who had been enslaved received nothing.

That is the first thing worth knowing about emancipation: it was won, slowly, by people who resisted, organised and revolted for decades. It was not a gift handed down on a single morning.

## Why Ghana marks it

Walk along the coast from Accra to Takoradi and you pass the reason. Cape Coast Castle, Elmina Castle and dozens of smaller forts were the warehouses of the Atlantic slave trade. Millions of people were held in their dungeons before being forced through the "Door of No Return" onto ships bound for the Americas.

Ghana added Emancipation Day to its national calendar in 1998, linking the Caribbean's 1 August to the places where the journey began. That same year, the remains of two people who had been enslaved in the Americas, known as Samuel Carson and Crystal, were brought back from the United States and Jamaica and reburied at Assin Manso in the Central Region.

Assin Manso was chosen for a reason. It was a slave market, and the river there was where captives were washed before the final march to the coast. People still call it the "last bath": the last time they touched water on African soil.

Since then, Emancipation Day in Ghana has become a week of wreath-layings, vigils and reunions, often tied to PANAFEST, with people from the diaspora coming home to the coast their ancestors were taken from.

## Emancipation, plural

The first of August is only one of several emancipation days.

In the United States, Abraham Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation took effect on 1 January 1863, though it only applied to the Confederate states then at war with the Union. Slavery was abolished nationwide by the Thirteenth Amendment in December 1865. And on 19 June 1865, Union soldiers arrived in Galveston, Texas, to announce that the enslaved people there were free, more than two years after the proclamation. That day, Juneteenth, became a federal holiday in 2021.

Different dates, same pattern: freedom announced by law, delayed in practice, and fought for on the ground long before and long after.

## What it still asks

In 2019, Ghana's Year of Return marked 400 years since enslaved Africans were recorded arriving in the English colony of Virginia, and thousands of people from the diaspora visited, some for the first time. Emancipation Day carries the same invitation every year: to look at the forts on Ghana's coast not just as tourist sites, but as evidence.

Emancipation is not only something that happened in 1834 or 1865. It is the ongoing work of making sure that freedom on paper becomes freedom in fact: in who gets educated, who gets hired, who gets heard. The castles are still standing. So is the question they ask.
