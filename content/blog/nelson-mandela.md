Two numbers sit at the centre of Nelson Mandela's life: 27 and 5. Twenty-seven years in prison. Five years as president. The first is about what he endured. The second is about what he chose.

## Before prison

Mandela was born on 18 July 1918 in Mvezo, in what is now South Africa's Eastern Cape. He trained as a lawyer and, in the 1940s, helped found the African National Congress Youth League, which pushed the ANC towards mass action.

In 1948 the National Party came to power and turned segregation into a full legal system: apartheid. Laws decided where Black South Africans could live, work, travel and go to school. Mandela and the ANC campaigned against it through defiance campaigns and boycotts, and in 1956 he and more than a hundred others were charged with treason. The trial dragged on for years and ended in acquittal.

After police killed 69 people at Sharpeville in 1960 and the ANC was banned, Mandela helped form an armed wing, Umkhonto we Sizwe. He was arrested in 1962.

## Twenty-seven years

At the Rivonia trial in 1964, facing a possible death sentence, Mandela spoke from the dock for hours. He described the free and democratic society he hoped for and ended by saying it was an ideal for which he was prepared to die. He was sentenced to life imprisonment.

He spent 18 of the next years on Robben Island, breaking rocks in a lime quarry, followed by years in Pollsmoor Prison and Victor Verster Prison. Outside, the campaign to free him grew into a global movement, along with boycotts and sanctions against the apartheid state.

On 11 February 1990, after 27 years, he walked out of Victor Verster Prison a free man.

## Five years

What happened next surprised many people. Instead of revenge, Mandela negotiated, with the very government that had imprisoned him. In 1993 he and President F. W. de Klerk shared the Nobel Peace Prize for ending apartheid peacefully. On 27 April 1994, South Africans of all races voted together for the first time, and Mandela became president.

His government created the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, where victims and perpetrators of apartheid-era abuses gave testimony in public. Then, in 1999, after a single term, he stepped down. On a continent where too many leaders have refused to leave, he left.

## The debate he left behind

Mandela died on 5 December 2013 in Johannesburg. South Africans still debate his legacy. Some argue that reconciliation came at the cost of economic justice, and that the wealth gap apartheid created was left largely intact. Others say a peaceful transition was the only foundation anything else could have been built on. Both arguments deserve to be heard.

What isn't up for debate is the example of the five. Power is something you can hold and then hand over. Mandela showed that the handing over can be its own act of leadership, and maybe the hardest one.
