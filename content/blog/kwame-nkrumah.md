Just after midnight on 6 March 1957, at the Old Polo Grounds in Accra, Kwame Nkrumah told a crowd that Ghana was free, forever. The Gold Coast, a British colony for more than eight decades, had become Ghana, among the very first countries in sub-Saharan Africa to win its independence from a European power.

That night is the part of the story everyone knows. The road to it, and what came after, is worth knowing too.

## The road to the Polo Grounds

Nkrumah was born in 1909 in Nkroful, in the Western Region, and trained as a teacher at Achimota. In 1935 he went to the United States, where he studied at Lincoln University and the University of Pennsylvania, then on to London. In 1945 he helped organise the Fifth Pan-African Congress in Manchester, where a generation of future African leaders argued that independence should be demanded, not requested.

He came home in 1947 to work for the United Gold Coast Convention, then broke away in 1949 to found the Convention People's Party under a blunt slogan: "Self-government now." When his campaign of strikes and boycotts led to his arrest, the CPP won the 1951 election while he was still in prison. The British governor released him to lead the government. Six years later, the Union Jack came down.

## Independence was never meant to stop at the border

For Nkrumah, Ghana's freedom was the beginning, not the goal. He argued that Ghana's independence meant little unless the whole continent was liberated too, and he acted like it: hosting the All-African People's Conference in Accra in 1958, backing liberation movements, and pushing for a united Africa. When the Organisation of African Unity was founded in Addis Ababa in 1963, he argued for going much further, towards a single continental government. His book that year was titled, simply, *Africa Must Unite*.

The continent moved fast. In 1960 alone, seventeen African countries became independent.

At home he built at the same pace: schools, roads, the new port and township at Tema, and the Volta River Project, whose Akosombo Dam still powers much of the country.

## The harder chapters

An honest account has to include the rest. The Preventive Detention Act of 1958 let the government jail people without trial, and political opponents were imprisoned under it. In 1964 Ghana became a one-party state, with Nkrumah as president for life. The economy struggled under falling cocoa prices and rising debt.

On 24 February 1966, while he was abroad on a trip to Asia, the military and police overthrew his government. He never returned to Ghana alive. He lived in exile in Guinea, where Sékou Touré named him honorary co-president, and died in Bucharest on 27 April 1972. Today he lies in the Kwame Nkrumah Memorial Park in Accra.

## Why he still matters

Ghanaians still argue about Nkrumah, and that's healthy. He was a visionary who gave a continent its confidence, and a leader whose government silenced its critics. Both are true.

What has outlived the arguments is the idea at the centre of his work: that Africans can and should decide their own future, together. Every time someone builds something on this continent, for this continent, that idea is at work.
