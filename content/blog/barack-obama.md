On 11 July 2009, less than six months into his presidency, Barack Obama stood in Ghana's Parliament in Accra. Ghana was his first stop in sub-Saharan Africa as president, and he said he had chosen it because it was a working democracy.

The line from that speech that people here still quote is short: "Africa doesn't need strongmen, it needs strong institutions."

## Who he was by then

Obama was born on 4 August 1961 in Honolulu, Hawaii, to an American mother and a Kenyan father. He worked as a community organiser in Chicago, studied law at Harvard, where he became the first Black president of the *Harvard Law Review*, and served in the Illinois State Senate and then the US Senate.

In November 2008 he was elected the 44th president of the United States, the first African American to hold the office. In Ghana, as in much of the world, the result was celebrated in the streets.

## The Accra speech

The speech was warm, but it was not flattery. Obama argued that Africa's future was up to Africans, and that development depends on good governance: on courts, police and elections that work regardless of who is in charge. He named corruption and conflict as obstacles that no amount of aid could fix, and said that hating someone for their tribe or religion has no place in the 21st century.

Some listeners welcomed it as honesty from a friend. Others felt that being lectured on governance by a Western leader, even one with family on the continent, missed the role the West had played in Africa's history.

That afternoon, Obama took his wife and daughters to Cape Coast Castle and walked through the dungeons where enslaved Africans had been held before being shipped across the Atlantic. He said it was important for his daughters to see how cruel history can be.

## Eight years in office

Obama served two terms, from 2009 to 2017. Within his first year he was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize, a decision even many supporters thought premature. His signature domestic law was the Affordable Care Act of 2010, which expanded health insurance to millions of Americans.

For Africa, his administration launched the Young African Leaders Initiative in 2010, which later grew into the Mandela Washington Fellowship, and Power Africa in 2013, aimed at expanding access to electricity.

His record is debated from every direction. Supporters point to the economic recovery after the 2008 financial crisis and to the expansion of health coverage. Critics on the right opposed the Affordable Care Act and much of his agenda. Critics on the left and abroad faulted the expanded use of drone strikes. And some African observers felt his Africa policy fell short of the hopes his election had raised.

## Why the line lasts

Presidents leave. Governments change. The point of that sentence in Accra was that the things that last should not depend on any one person: not on a president, not on a party, not on a strongman.

As someone who builds software, I find that idea familiar. Good systems are the ones that keep working when the person who built them isn't in the room. The same is true of countries.
